# RSS Is Back — And This Time It Makes Sense

Most people assume RSS died sometime around 2013. That's the year Google killed Google Reader, and with it — the narrative goes — killed RSS as a mainstream way to read the internet.

The narrative is wrong. RSS didn't die. It went underground, kept alive by a committed community of users who never stopped using it and developers who never stopped building for it. And now, a decade later, it's genuinely coming back — not out of nostalgia, but because the conditions that pushed people away from RSS have reversed.

## A Brief History of the Format

RSS (Really Simple Syndication) emerged in the late 1990s as a simple idea: instead of visiting 50 websites every morning to check for new posts, you subscribe to their feeds and read everything in one place. The website pushes updates to you; you don't go looking.

By the mid-2000s, RSS was thriving. Google Reader launched in 2005 and quickly became the dominant feed reader, with millions of users. Bloggers published RSS feeds. News organizations provided them. Podcasts were (and still are) distributed via RSS. The format was a cornerstone of the open web — decentralized, user-controlled, with no intermediary deciding what you should see.

Then Google killed Reader in 2013, citing declining usage. The likely real reasons were more strategic: Google was betting on Google+ as its social layer, and an open protocol that gave users direct control over their information diet didn't serve that goal.

Mainstream RSS usage dropped sharply. Many publications stopped maintaining their feeds. The story hardened: RSS was a niche technology for nerds, superseded by social media's algorithmic feeds.

## The Quiet Decade

What actually happened between 2013 and now is more interesting than the "RSS died" story suggests.

Feedbin, Feedly, Inoreader, NewsBlur, Miniflux, and dozens of other readers absorbed Google Reader's displaced users. These services were smaller but often better — more focused, more respectful of your attention, maintained by teams that genuinely believed in the format. The user base shrank but became more committed.

Meanwhile, RSS kept functioning as invisible infrastructure. Every podcast in the world distributes via RSS. YouTube channels have RSS feeds. Reddit subreddits have RSS feeds. Most blogs built on WordPress, Ghost, or Hugo generate RSS feeds automatically. The format never went away — it just stopped being talked about.

## Why RSS Is Surging Now

The current resurgence isn't driven by a single cause. It's a convergence of several trends, each of which makes the case for RSS stronger than it's been in years.

### Algorithmic Feed Fatigue

The social media platforms that replaced RSS as most people's reading layer have optimized themselves into a corner. Facebook, Instagram, Twitter/X, and TikTok all use algorithmic feeds that prioritize engagement over relevance. The result is feeds full of rage bait, viral content from strangers, and ads — with the content you actually subscribed to buried somewhere in between.

People are increasingly aware of this dynamic and actively looking for alternatives. RSS offers exactly what algorithmic feeds took away: a chronological list of content from sources you chose, with nothing inserted, reordered, or hidden by anyone else.

### Platform Instability

Twitter's transformation into X was a wake-up call for anyone who had built their reading life around a single platform. Overnight, API access was restricted, third-party clients were killed, and the platform's character changed fundamentally.

For content creators, the lesson was clear: don't build your audience on rented land. For readers, the lesson was equally clear: don't let a single company control your information diet. RSS is the antidote to both — a protocol that no single entity owns.

### Newsletter Fatigue

Email newsletters surged as an alternative to social media, with platforms like Substack and Beehiiv enabling thousands of independent writers. But newsletters have their own problems: they clutter your inbox, they're hard to organize, they arrive on the sender's schedule, and there's no good way to skim and triage them.

Many newsletter platforms now offer RSS feeds as an alternative delivery method. Readers get the content without the inbox pollution — and increasingly, they're choosing that option.

### The Indie Web Renaissance

There's a broader movement happening: a return to personal websites, blogs, and independently hosted content. Fueled partly by frustration with platforms and partly by tools that make self-publishing easier than ever, more people are starting blogs and personal sites. And blogs come with RSS feeds by default.

The Hacker News and indie web communities have been especially active in this revival — building and sharing RSS tools, curating feed lists, and advocating for the open web. It's not a mass movement yet, but it's growing faster than it has in a decade.

## RSS as Trust Infrastructure

Here's where things get genuinely interesting. RSS by itself is a content delivery mechanism — it tells you that a source published something new, but it doesn't tell you whether that source is worth reading. Add a trust layer, and RSS becomes something qualitatively different.

Consider the difference between two reading experiences:

**Traditional RSS:** You subscribe to feeds. You read what they publish. If a source becomes unreliable, you eventually notice and unsubscribe. The burden of quality evaluation falls entirely on you.

**Trust-enhanced RSS:** Your feeds are filtered and augmented by community trust signals. Domains with low trust scores are flagged. Content from highly trusted sources is surfaced. Your social graph's reading habits inform what you see. The burden of quality evaluation is shared across a network of real humans whose judgment you trust.

This is what Voucha builds on top of RSS infrastructure. The feeds provide the content pipeline — a decentralized, user-controlled flow of information from across the web. Community-voted domain authority and social graph curation provide the trust layer that helps you navigate that flow without drowning in it.

The combination addresses the two main criticisms RSS has always faced:

1. **"There's too much to read"** — Trust signals help you prioritize. Content from domains your network vouches for rises to the top.

2. **"I miss important things from sources I haven't subscribed to"** — Social graph curation surfaces content from outside your explicit subscriptions, based on what trusted people in your network are reading.

## What's Different This Time

Previous RSS revivals were driven primarily by nostalgia and nerd enthusiasm. This one is different because the conditions that drove people away from RSS have actually reversed.

In 2013, social media feeds were good enough. They showed you content from people and sources you followed, with modest algorithmic tweaking. The trade-off — giving up some control in exchange for convenience — felt reasonable.

In 2025, that trade-off has collapsed. Social media feeds are optimized for the platform's revenue, not your information needs. The convenience of everything in one place now comes at the cost of having everything polluted by engagement optimization, advertising, and algorithmic manipulation.

RSS offers an exit from that. Not a retreat to 2005 — the format and tools have genuinely evolved — but a return to a simple principle: you decide what you read, from sources you chose, in the order they were published, with no algorithm deciding what you should see or what should be hidden from you.

## The Tooling Has Caught Up

Modern RSS readers like NetNewsWire, Miniflux, and Reeder offer polished experiences that rival any social media app. Browser extensions make subscribing to feeds trivial. Self-hosted options give technically inclined users complete control. Standards like JSON Feed have modernized the format itself.

On the publishing side, every major CMS generates RSS feeds automatically. Podcasts are universally distributed via RSS. Even platforms that don't prominently advertise RSS support often expose feeds — YouTube, Reddit, GitHub, and many others.

The infrastructure has been there all along. What's changed is that people now have real reasons to use it — not because RSS is retro or cool, but because the alternatives have become actively hostile to anyone who wants to read things they actually chose to read.

RSS isn't making a comeback because it's nostalgic. It's making a comeback because it's right.
