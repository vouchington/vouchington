# Why G2 and Capterra Reviews Can't Be Trusted (And What to Use Instead)

If you've ever selected B2B software based on G2 or Capterra reviews, you've participated in a system that's optimized for vendor revenue, not buyer accuracy.

This isn't a conspiracy theory. It's the observable result of a business model where the reviewed companies are also the paying customers.

## The B2B Review Problem

### G2: Badges for Sale

G2's quarterly "Grid Reports" categorize software into Leaders, High Performers, Contenders, and Niche players. These badges are prominently displayed on vendor websites and in sales decks. They look like independent assessments.

The methodology behind these badges factors in "market presence" — which correlates with how much a vendor engages with the G2 platform, encourages employees and customers to leave reviews, and pays for G2 premium features. Smaller companies with excellent products and satisfied users but limited G2 engagement routinely appear in lower quadrants than larger companies with more resources to invest in the G2 ecosystem.

The badges are not fabricated. But they reflect G2 engagement as much as product quality, and the distinction is invisible to buyers who see a "Leader" badge on a vendor's website.

### Capterra: Paid Placement Disguised as Ranking

Capterra is more transparent about its model — vendors pay for placement in category listings. But the practical effect for buyers is the same: the products you see first are not necessarily the best products. They're the products whose vendors paid for visibility.

Capterra reviews themselves are generally real, but the context around them (placement, featured status, category ranking) is commercially influenced. A buyer casually browsing Capterra categories will naturally give more attention to top-listed products, which are top-listed because of payment, not quality.

### Incentivized Reviews

Both G2 and Capterra facilitate review collection campaigns where vendors offer incentives (gift cards, donations to charity, in-product benefits) to customers who leave reviews. The result is predictable: incentivized reviewers skew positive. They're not lying — most are genuinely satisfied users. But they're a self-selected sample of the most engaged, most incentivized customers. Dissatisfied users and users who quietly churned are underrepresented.

Review volume itself becomes gamed. A vendor that runs an aggressive review collection campaign can accumulate hundreds of 4.5-star reviews, burying the handful of authentic negative reviews from users who had genuinely bad experiences.

### The Vendor-Reviewer Power Dynamic

In B2B, reviewers are often identifiable. Reviews on G2 show the reviewer's company and title. This creates a chilling effect: a buyer who leaves a critical review of a vendor they still use risks damaging a business relationship. The incentive is to either leave a positive review or leave no review at all.

## What Honest SaaS Reviews Look Like

Contrast the incentivized, identified, vendor-facilitated reviews on G2 with what genuinely useful B2B software intelligence would contain:

### Actual Usage Duration

"How long have you actually used this product?" is one of the most important signals in software evaluation. A review from someone who's used a tool for 2 weeks tells you about the onboarding experience. A review from someone who's used it for 2 years tells you about long-term reliability, scaling issues, and vendor relationship quality.

Most G2 reviews don't meaningfully distinguish between these timeframes. On Voucha, usage duration is a structured field that's visible on every review and data point.

### Migration Difficulty

One of the highest-value questions in B2B software: "How hard is it to switch away from this product?" Lock-in is a feature vendors build deliberately, but lock-in data is something no vendor will publish.

Structured data points tracking migrations — what you switched from, what you switched to, how long the migration took, what data was lost or degraded — create a migration difficulty index that helps buyers assess not just whether a product is good, but whether they can leave if it stops being good.

### Hidden Costs

The pricing page says $50 per user per month. What it doesn't say:

- SSO costs an extra $100/month (the "SSO tax")
- API access requires the enterprise tier at 3x the price
- Integrations with your existing tools require a paid connector
- Storage limits that seemed generous at evaluation become expensive at scale
- Professional services for implementation that weren't quoted upfront

Data from real customers who've experienced the full cost stack reveals the true cost of ownership, not the marketing-page price.

### Real Support Experiences

Vendor websites showcase "24/7 support" and "dedicated customer success." What users actually report often looks different:

- Response times that increase dramatically after the initial contract period
- Support quality that varies based on contract size
- Feature requests that are acknowledged but never implemented
- Account managers who change quarterly

Reviews that capture support responsiveness, issue resolution rates, and account management quality over time provide a far more accurate picture than the support section of a vendor's pricing page.

### Switch Patterns

The most damning signal for a B2B product: "What percentage of users switch away within 12 months, and where do they go?"

This data is invisible on existing review platforms. No vendor publishes churn data. But when users share data points tracking their tool switches — including what they switched from, what they switched to, and why — the aggregate reveals real adoption and retention patterns.

"35% of users who adopted Tool X switched to Tool Y within 18 months, citing integration limitations and pricing" is intelligence that can save a company months of failed adoption and significant switching costs.

## Making Better Software Decisions

The next time you're evaluating B2B software, consider what data you're actually looking at:

- Is the ranking based on product quality or vendor spending?
- Are the reviews from incentivized campaigns or organic users?
- Do you know how long reviewers have actually used the product?
- Can you see what products users switched away from — and to?
- Are the reviews from people whose judgment you trust, or anonymous strangers?

If you can't answer these questions, you're making a decision with less information than you think. Structured, trust-weighted, community-sourced reviews exist to fill that gap.
