# What Is Credit Card Churning? A Beginner's Guide

Credit card churning is the practice of repeatedly opening credit cards to earn sign-up bonuses, then moving on to the next card. Done responsibly, it can generate thousands of dollars in travel rewards or cash back each year. Done recklessly, it can damage your credit and lead to debt.

This guide covers the fundamentals: how churning works, who should consider it, the risks involved, and the terminology you need to navigate the community.

## How Credit Card Churning Works

The core mechanic is simple. Credit card issuers offer sign-up bonuses (SUBs) to attract new customers. These bonuses typically dwarf the rewards you'd earn from everyday spending. A card that earns 2% cash back on $30,000 in annual spending yields $600 per year. The same card's sign-up bonus might be worth $750 after spending just $4,000 in three months.

Churners exploit this gap systematically:

1. **Apply** for a card with a valuable sign-up bonus
2. **Meet the minimum spending requirement** (MSR) within the required timeframe
3. **Collect the bonus** once the spending requirement is met
4. **Repeat** with the next card, either keeping or canceling the first card after the annual fee hits

The math is compelling. A disciplined churner opening four to six cards per year can realistically earn $3,000 to $8,000 in annual value, depending on the bonuses available and their spending capacity.

## Key Terminology

Before diving deeper, here are the terms you'll encounter constantly in churning communities:

| Term                | Meaning                                                                                 |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **SUB**             | Sign-up bonus. The reward you earn for meeting initial spending requirements.           |
| **MSR**             | Minimum spending requirement. The amount you must charge to the card to earn the SUB.   |
| **5/24**            | Chase's rule limiting approvals if you've opened 5+ personal cards in 24 months.        |
| **AF**              | Annual fee. The yearly cost of holding a card.                                          |
| **PC**              | Product change. Converting one card to another within the same issuer.                  |
| **DP**              | Data point. A reported outcome (approval, denial, bonus receipt) from a real applicant. |
| **MR / UR / TYP**   | Amex Membership Rewards / Chase Ultimate Rewards / Citi ThankYou Points.                |
| **P2**              | Player 2. A partner or spouse who also churns, doubling household earning potential.    |
| **MS**              | Manufactured spending. Techniques to meet MSR without organic purchases.                |
| **Retention offer** | A bonus offered by the issuer to keep you from canceling.                               |

## Who Should Consider Churning

Churning isn't for everyone. It works best for people who meet all of the following criteria:

- **Good to excellent credit** (typically 720+ FICO). Most premium cards require strong credit histories.
- **Organized and disciplined.** You need to track application dates, spending deadlines, and annual fee dates across multiple cards.
- **No tendency to overspend.** If having available credit tempts you to spend beyond your means, churning will cost you far more than it earns.
- **Sufficient organic spending** to meet MSRs, or willingness to learn manufactured spending techniques.
- **No major credit needs in the near future.** If you're applying for a mortgage or auto loan within 6 to 12 months, new inquiries and accounts can complicate the process.

## The Risks

Churning carries real risks that forums and influencers sometimes downplay:

**Credit score impact.** Each application generates a hard inquiry (typically -5 to -10 points temporarily) and reduces your average age of accounts. For most churners, scores recover within a few months, but the short-term dip is real.

**Issuer restrictions.** Banks have caught on. Chase's 5/24 rule, Amex's once-per-lifetime bonus language, and Citi's 48-month rules all limit how often you can earn bonuses. Getting shut down — having all your accounts closed by an issuer — is a real, if uncommon, outcome.

**Annual fees.** Premium cards often carry $95 to $695 annual fees. If you forget to cancel or downgrade before the fee posts, it eats into your bonus value.

**Tax implications.** Referral bonuses and some bank account bonuses may be taxable income. Sign-up bonuses from credit card spending are generally considered rebates and are not taxable, but consult a tax professional for your situation.

**Temptation to overspend.** Meeting a $5,000 MSR in three months can push people to buy things they wouldn't otherwise purchase. If you're paying interest on those purchases, you've already lost.

## A Basic First Strategy

If you're starting from zero, a common beginner approach looks like this:

1. **Check your credit score.** If it's below 700, focus on building credit before churning. A secured card or a simple cash-back card used responsibly for 6 to 12 months can get you there.

2. **Start with Chase.** Because of the 5/24 rule, Chase cards should generally come first. The Chase Sapphire Preferred or Chase Freedom Flex are common starting points.

3. **Plan your spending.** Before applying, map out how you'll meet the MSR with purchases you'd make anyway: rent (if your landlord accepts cards), insurance premiums, groceries, utilities.

4. **Apply and track.** Note the approval date, the MSR amount, the deadline, and when the annual fee will hit. A simple spreadsheet works.

5. **Meet the MSR, collect the bonus, evaluate.** Once the bonus posts, decide whether the card's ongoing rewards justify keeping it. If not, downgrade to a no-annual-fee version or cancel after the first year.

6. **Move to the next card.** Space applications 2 to 3 months apart to manage inquiries and avoid raising red flags with issuers.

## Why Community Data Points Matter

Here's where churning gets interesting — and where many beginners make their first mistake: relying on outdated blog posts instead of current data.

Credit card issuers change their rules constantly. Blog posts written 18 months ago may describe approval criteria, bonus amounts, or application rules that no longer apply. What matters is what's happening right now, to real applicants.

This is where data points come in. A data point is a report from someone who applied for a card — their credit score, number of recent accounts, income, and whether they were approved or denied. When you have hundreds or thousands of those reports together, patterns emerge that no single editorial review can match:

- What credit score ranges are actually getting approved for a given card
- Whether a particular issuer is enforcing a rule strictly or loosely this month
- How long it takes for bonuses to post after meeting the MSR
- Whether retention offers are available and what they look like

The difference between a churner who reads data points and one who doesn't is the difference between applying strategically and applying blind. When you can see that 87% of applicants with a 740+ score and fewer than 3 recent inquiries were approved for a specific card, you can make informed decisions instead of guessing.

## Getting Started

The best time to start is when you have a specific goal. Maybe it's a free flight to Europe, a hotel stay for a family vacation, or simply maximizing cash back on spending you're already doing.

Define your goal, research which bonuses align with it, check what people with profiles like yours are actually seeing, and apply strategically. Start with one card. See how it feels. Track your results. Then decide whether to continue.

Churning rewards patience and organization. The people who succeed at it aren't the ones who apply for everything at once. They're the ones who plan three cards ahead, understand the rules each issuer enforces, and use what the community has learned to make every application count.
