# Credit Score Myths Debunked by Real Data

Credit scores influence everything from loan interest rates to apartment applications, yet they remain one of the most misunderstood aspects of personal finance. Conventional wisdom is rife with myths — many of which persist because they were once partially true, or because they sound intuitively correct.

What real people actually report, collected across thousands of credit profiles and outcomes, tells a different story. This guide examines the most persistent credit score myths and what the data actually shows.

## Myth 1: Closing a Credit Card Will Destroy Your Score

**The myth:** Closing a credit card tanks your credit score immediately and significantly.

**What the data shows:** Closing a card can affect your score through two mechanisms: increasing your credit utilization ratio (if you carry balances on other cards) and eventually reducing your average age of accounts. But the impact is usually modest and temporary.

Reports from people who closed cards show:

- **Immediate impact:** Typically 0 to 15 points for most people. The effect is larger if closing the card significantly increases your utilization ratio (the percentage of available credit you're using).
- **No immediate age-of-accounts impact:** Closed accounts remain on your credit report for up to 10 years and continue to age. The average age of accounts isn't affected until the closed account eventually falls off your report.
- **Recovery timeline:** Most reports show scores recovering to pre-closure levels within 1 to 3 months, assuming utilization isn't permanently elevated.

**The takeaway:** If you're paying an annual fee on a card you don't use and the issuer won't waive it, closing (or downgrading to a no-fee version) is usually the right financial decision. The score impact is minor for most profiles.

## Myth 2: Checking Your Own Credit Score Hurts It

**The myth:** Any credit check lowers your score, including checking your own.

**What the data shows:** This myth conflates two fundamentally different types of inquiries:

- **Soft inquiries** (checking your own score, pre-approval checks, employer background checks): These appear on your credit report but have absolutely zero impact on your score. You can check your score daily without consequence.
- **Hard inquiries** (credit card applications, loan applications): These can temporarily reduce your score by 5 to 10 points and remain on your report for 2 years, though their scoring impact diminishes after about 12 months.

Reported score changes consistently confirm this distinction. Among thousands of tracked outcomes, self-checks never correlate with score decreases. The myth likely persists because the difference between hard and soft inquiries isn't intuitive to people unfamiliar with the system.

**The takeaway:** Check your score as often as you like. The evidence is unanimous: it has no effect.

## Myth 3: You Need to Carry a Balance to Build Credit

**The myth:** Paying your credit card in full every month doesn't help your credit score. You need to carry a balance and pay interest to show lenders you're a good borrower.

**What the data shows:** This is perhaps the most damaging myth in personal finance because it costs people real money. Reports from thousands of credit profiles show:

- People who pay in full every month build credit at the same rate (or faster) than those who carry balances
- The scoring models care about whether you make payments on time and how much of your available credit you're using at the time your statement is reported — not whether you pay interest
- Carrying a balance costs you interest without any scoring benefit

The confusion arises because a reported balance of $0 across all cards can sometimes produce a slightly lower score than a small reported balance. But this doesn't mean you need to pay interest. Your statement balance (what's reported) and your carried balance (what you pay interest on) are different things. If you use your card and pay the full statement balance by the due date, a balance is reported to the bureaus and you pay zero interest.

**The takeaway:** Always pay your statement balance in full. The idea that carrying a balance helps your score is a myth that benefits credit card issuers, not you.

## Myth 4: All Hard Inquiries Are Equal

**The myth:** Every hard inquiry has the same impact on your score.

**What the data shows:** The scoring models treat inquiries differently depending on context:

- **Rate shopping protection:** Multiple inquiries for the same type of loan (mortgage, auto, student) within a 14 to 45 day window (depending on the scoring model) are typically counted as a single inquiry. The models recognize that you're comparison shopping, not seeking multiple loans.
- **Credit card inquiries** don't receive this bundling treatment. Each application counts separately.
- **Impact varies by profile.** Reports consistently show that the same inquiry has a larger score impact on thin credit files (few accounts, short history) than on thick files. Someone with 15 accounts and a 10-year history might lose 2 points from an inquiry, while someone with 2 accounts and a 1-year history might lose 10 points.

Reports tracking score changes after inquiries consistently show that the impact is almost always recovered within 3 to 6 months, and inquiries stop affecting scores entirely after 12 months — even though they remain visible on your report for 24 months.

**The takeaway:** Don't avoid applying for credit you need just because of inquiry fear. But for credit cards specifically, be strategic about spacing applications since each one counts separately.

## Myth 5: Income Affects Your Credit Score

**The myth:** Higher income means a higher credit score.

**What the data shows:** Income is not a factor in any credit scoring model. FICO and VantageScore do not receive or use income data. Your score is based on:

1. Payment history (35% of FICO)
2. Credit utilization (30%)
3. Length of credit history (15%)
4. Credit mix (10%)
5. New credit/inquiries (10%)

Income appears nowhere in this calculation. A person earning $40,000 with perfect payment history and low utilization will outscore a person earning $400,000 with late payments and maxed-out cards.

However, income indirectly matters because it affects approval decisions and credit limits. Higher credit limits mean lower utilization ratios at the same spending levels. And higher income makes it easier to pay bills on time. But the score itself is income-blind.

When you control for payment history and utilization, there's no correlation between reported income and credit score.

**The takeaway:** Income helps you get credit and manage it, but your score doesn't know or care what you earn.

## Myth 6: Paying Off Collections Immediately Improves Your Score

**The myth:** Paying a collection account removes it from your report and immediately boosts your score.

**What the data shows:** This depends heavily on which scoring model is being used:

- **FICO 9 and VantageScore 3.0+** ignore paid collections entirely. Paying off a collection under these models does remove its scoring impact.
- **FICO 8** (still widely used for credit card decisions) does not distinguish between paid and unpaid collections. A paid collection still hurts your score under FICO 8.
- **Newer FICO versions (10, 10T)** vary in their treatment.

People who paid collections report widely varying results — which makes sense given the different models. Some report 30+ point increases. Others report no change at all. The difference almost always traces back to which scoring model the person was monitoring.

**The takeaway:** Paying collections is generally the right thing to do (and some lenders require it regardless of score impact), but don't expect an automatic score boost. The impact depends on the scoring model being used, which varies by lender.

## Myth 7: You Only Have One Credit Score

**The myth:** There's a single credit score number that all lenders see.

**What the data shows:** You have dozens of credit scores. The three major bureaus (Experian, Equifax, TransUnion) each maintain separate reports with potentially different information. And multiple scoring models are applied to each report:

- FICO 8 (most common for credit cards)
- FICO 9
- FICO 2, 4, 5 (commonly used for mortgages)
- FICO Auto Score (used for auto loans)
- VantageScore 3.0, 4.0
- Proprietary issuer scores

It's common for the same person to have a FICO 8 of 780 and a VantageScore of 750, or to have a 30-point difference between their Experian and TransUnion FICO scores.

This matters because the score you see on Credit Karma (VantageScore) is often not the score a credit card issuer uses (FICO 8). When people share what score they checked alongside their application outcome, it helps bridge this gap — revealing the practical approval thresholds for each card, with the right scoring model in context.

**The takeaway:** Know which score you're looking at and which score your target lender uses. Application outcomes that include the scoring model used are far more useful than those that don't.

## Myth 8: Credit Score Is the Only Factor in Approval Decisions

**The myth:** If your score is high enough, you'll be approved.

**What the data shows:** Credit score is one input among many. Reports consistently show denials for applicants with scores well above typical thresholds. Common non-score reasons for denial include:

- Too many recent inquiries or new accounts (regardless of score)
- Insufficient income relative to existing credit obligations
- Too many cards with the specific issuer
- Velocity (opening too many cards too quickly)
- Too short a credit history (even with a high score from limited data)
- Issuer-specific rules (Chase 5/24, Amex once-per-lifetime, etc.)

This is where real approval data is uniquely valuable: it captures these multi-dimensional denial reasons. When you can filter for applicants with your exact score, similar account count, and similar inquiry history, you get a much more realistic picture than score alone would provide.

**The takeaway:** A good score is necessary but not sufficient. Use real application outcomes to understand the full picture of what a specific issuer looks for.

## How to Use Community Data for Your Score

Credit scores respond to specific actions in predictable ways, and reported outcomes put real numbers to those responses:

- **Paying down utilization** from 50% to under 10%: average increases of 30 to 60 points, often within one billing cycle
- **Removing an authorized user account** you didn't request: variable impact, but 0 to 20 points in most reports
- **Waiting for inquiries to age past 12 months:** typically 5 to 15 points recovered per inquiry

These patterns only become visible when many people report the same action and its result. Individual anecdotes are unreliable. What the community has collectively experienced is not perfect, but it's the best tool available for understanding how scores actually work versus how people think they work.

The myths persist because they're simple and the truth is complicated. Real data doesn't simplify the truth, but it does make it visible. And visible truth, even when complex, is more useful than a comforting myth.
